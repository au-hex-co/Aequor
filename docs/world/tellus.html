    World/Tellus

    Tellus

    worlddevelopingupdated 2026-09-08#world#deity#malum

    One of the three first beings, dragon of the earth. Along with Anima and Aequor, Tellus helped create Primum, the first man, and gave him the gift of duty. His Ichor — his own magic, not his blood — runs silver, the color Nexum draws from him; see Nexum for how it combines with Anima's gold once both are loosed together.

    Fate

    Tellus was betrayed by the very humanity he gave duty to — the event known as the Malum, so total it splits the entire calendar into Before Malum (BM) and After Malum (AM). See Timeline of the Aetas.

    The betrayal starts small: Tellus once told the five-year-old Nexus "I'll protect you, child." Hot-headed as ever, he later loses his temper with the boy and moves to restrain him — breaking two things at once, from two different directions. Restraining Nexus violates the boy's own agency, triggering the dormant Sacrarium residue every son of Vesta's line carries (see Sacrarium) — an assault on Tellus from outside. And breaking the oath violates Tellus himself from within: he is not merely subject to duty and oath, he is what they are, the source fate drew that gift from before it was ever given to Primum. Nothing outranks him, so nothing could have stopped him from breaking his own word — but nothing can violate what it is and remain whole, either. Part of who he is simply stops being true. Neither force alone would have ended him; it is the sustained outside assault, layered on the internal unraveling he cannot simply stop, that becomes the first oath-breaking backlash the world has ever felt, and it rips Tellus apart.

    Rather than die outright, Tellus's survival instinct takes over: he shelters his essence inside Nexus — the one place the Sacrarium's own attack cannot follow, since it answers to the boy, not to him. It is a ditch effort, not a discovered power; nothing else can do this, and he does not get to do it again. The fusion is also what makes Nexus ageless — the first Fae King — and what leaves Tellus bound inside him for the centuries it takes to reach the Aetas of Manus, still coming apart the entire time rather than simply lying dormant.

    Nexus is finally killed centuries later — not by force, but by his own hand as much as anyone else's: a promise he'd been straining against on purpose, to fight what Tellus had become inside him, finally breaks outright in battle, and the self-inflicted collapse that follows opens him to the one weapon that could kill him — a spear tipped with Anima's own scale, gold, wielded by an unnamed hand (see Nexus). In the wound, Tellus's silver Ichor fights to heal his host — pure survival instinct, the same reflex that started all of this — and loses. Anima's gold wins the body; the same collapse frees Tellus from it.

    Tellus is set loose again. He does not come back merely weakened. He comes back half mad: an unraveling that never stopped for the centuries he spent inside Nexus, not a fresh wound. What he sheds from Nexus's death alongside him is Nexum: duty's domain, once Tellus's alone to embody, now loosed on the world as the law of binding oaths — born from the very oath only he could have broken.

    Freed and ravaged, Tellus rampages through the age of disaster that follows, until Aequor lands the final blow — the same role Aequor already played once before, tearing out Anima's heart at his own request (see Lore in order, Chapter 4). This time there is no fusion left to escape into: Tellus dies for good.

    • Aetas of Terra (774–542 BM): worship shifted specifically to Tellus.
    • Aetas of Manus (541–0 BM): the age of disaster — from Nexus's death and Tellus's release to Tellus's final death at Aequor's hand, which marks the Malum.

    In Gameplay

    The last of Tellus's own blood, spilled at the Malum, survives as the relic Nectaris — the game's proof of concept for turning myth into mechanic. Its "Divine tier" ability lets Tellus, not the player, briefly act through the ship, echoing the Malum in miniature.

    His mountains are said to be the by-product of 108 years of divine boredom — "the land was never natural, only discarded godhood" (see Nectaris — The Mountain Was Once Flesh).
