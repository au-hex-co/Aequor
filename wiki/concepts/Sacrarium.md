# Sacrarium

**Type:** Inherited trait / bodily gift, carried by all fertile women descended from Vesta **Origin:** Given by [[Tellus]] and [[Aequor]] (the Two) to Vesta, now passed down through her line **Domain:** Agency, free breath, inherited will

## Description

Originally a round, smooth orb that weighed no more than a feather, containing a faint golden glint — the same golden light that once burned in [[Anima]]'s eye, extinguished the moment his heart was torn from him in the sealing of his kingdom. Vesta consumed the orb whole, and it took root in her.

From Vesta onward, every fertile woman of her line carries a piece of the Sacrarium within her — a fragment of Anima's heart passed down through the blood. When she bears a child, she gives up a chunk of her own share of it, so that the child is born already carrying agency, rather than needing it granted.

## Origin

After Anima's heart was ripped from him by Aequor's talon, as the price for granting agency beyond a single creation, Anima's kingdom of clouds sealed itself away from the world. Sometime after, Tellus and Aequor — now only Two — carried what remained of that sacrifice to [[Vesta]], shaped into an orb.

When Tellus was asked what the orb was, he looked at its golden glint and answered with a single word:

> "Sacrarium."

## Function

The Sacrarium was given so that Vesta's children, and every generation after, might breathe of their own volition — carrying agency in their blood rather than requiring it be granted, creation by creation, as it had been for [[Ferus]].

Vesta ate the orb three days after receiving it, urged on by [[Primum]]. Upon consuming it:

- She felt wind rustle through her hair, and heard whispers carried on it — the last trace of Anima's freedom passing into her, settling into her body as something she now carried rather than something given once.
- The nature of her union with Primum changed; for the next ten days she craved food rather than showing signs of pregnancy.
- Two months later, she first felt life within her.
- The child she eventually bore — carried three times longer than Ferus, and delivered in agony far beyond that first birth — inherited agency at birth, a piece of the Sacrarium given up from Vesta's own share, without needing it granted directly by a god.

Every fertile woman born of Vesta's line carries the Sacrarium the same way — not as a single gift spent once, but as something held in the body and passed on in pieces. A mother gives up a portion of her own Sacrarium at the moment each child is born, and it settles into any daughter born of her — the piece that gives the child agency of her own, carried forward rather than granted anew.

## Inheritance

Beyond what is passed at birth, the Sacrarium is bound to a woman for the rest of her life, and the remainder only moves on when she dies. On a woman's death, whatever Sacrarium she has left rushes all at once to her daughters.

If she has no surviving daughters, it instead flies to her closest living female relative, preferring youth over closeness of blood alone. The order runs roughly: niece, then sister, then a second niece, then mother, and so on outward — younger kin are favored ahead of older ones at a similar remove, so the Sacrarium tends to settle on whichever eligible woman has the most childbearing years still ahead of her, rather than simply the nearest relation.

This means a woman's portion is not fixed by birth alone: a woman may inherit more than once over her life, first receiving a portion at her own birth, then possibly more from her mother's death, and even a relative's remainder if her line runs short of daughters.

## Overflow and balance

A woman's capacity to hold Sacrarium has a limit. A woman who bears only one or two children, and so passes on very little, will find her own portion cap out — anything beyond that limit does not simply sit unused in her, it overflows outward into other branches of the family, reaching aunts, cousins, and kin well beyond her direct line.

This overflow is what keeps the Sacrarium from concentrating too heavily in any single bloodline or thinning out too far in another. Over generations, the system tends toward rough balance across the wider family and, by extension, across the world — no branch grows vastly more fertile than another for long, and no branch is left with nothing. Real variation still exists between individual women, but it evens out at the scale of families and generations rather than compounding endlessly in one direction.

## Infertility

Some women are born with very little Sacrarium — barely a trace inherited down the line. These women are infertile from birth. But depletion is not only inherited: a woman can also exhaust her own portion through childbearing itself, giving away a chunk with each child until nothing is left to give. A woman who bears enough children can become infertile in this way regardless of how much she started with.

Vesta herself, as goddess of fertility, decreed the rule plainly: a woman without any Sacrarium in her cannot give birth at all. Fertility is not a separate blessing granted by the gods case by case, as it once was for Vesta — it is entirely a matter of how much of the Sacrarium a woman carries, whether by birth or by what remains of her own.

Vesta alone stands outside this rule. She holds not a mere fragment but a god's heart in full, seeded further by the trees of the forest that saved her — making her the source the Sacrarium flows from, rather than merely another vessel for it.

## Theft

A man can take the Sacrarium from a woman by force — a violation counted among the gravest acts in this mythology, since it strips her not just of fertility but of the piece of Anima's freedom she carries and would otherwise pass to her own children or kin. What is stolen this way does not return to the woman it was taken from, nor does it pass down the line the way a natural death would allow; it is simply gone from where it belongs, taken rather than given.

## Notes

- Neither Vesta nor Primum are ever told, in plain terms, that the orb is Anima's heart. The reader is left to draw the line between the golden light fading from Anima's eye and the golden glint Tellus sees in the orb.
- The name itself (from _sacrarium_, "shrine" or "repository for sacred things") marks it as a vessel for something holy rather than an ordinary object — fitting for a god's heart repurposed as inheritance, and later as something carried in the body itself.
- Because each mother's share is finite and only ever given away, not restored, the Sacrarium carries the same refrain as the rest of the myth: _a price is always exacted_ — for Vesta first, and now for every mother after her, one child at a time.
- Open questions worth deciding, since theft now has real narrative weight:
    - How does theft actually happen — is it a ritual act, a specific kind of violence, or something a man can do unknowingly? This decides whether it reads as a targeted mythic transgression or an ever-present danger.
    - Does a woman who is stolen from simply become infertile, or does something worse happen to her — does it also cost her the years of life or vitality the Sacrarium was sustaining?
    - What happens to a stolen portion once a man holds it — does he keep it, does it curse him, does it decay outside a woman's body? Worth deciding if theft is ever going to be a plot event rather than background lore.
